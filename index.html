Rehearsal Room

Nerves before a pitch are not a character flaw — they are an untrained skill and an untrained body. This room trains both. Read the protocol, run the drill out loud, then climb the ladder one rung at a time.

The 90-second protocol

Run this in the parking lot, or in the sixty seconds before you click Join.

  1. Three physiological sighs. Two inhales through the nose — a short one, then a second sharp one to top off — followed by one long, slow exhale through the mouth. It is the fastest deliberate way to bring a racing heart rate down in real time.
  2. Say "I'm excited" out loud. Not "calm down." Anxiety and excitement are the same physical arousal wearing different labels, and relabeling costs nothing while suppressing costs everything. In the research, speakers told to get excited were rated more persuasive, competent and confident than the ones told to relax.
  3. Name one outcome in one sentence. "By the end of this call I want a walkthrough scheduled." Not "I want it to go well." A vague goal has no finish line, so your body never gets the all-clear.
  4. Stand up. Feet planted, shoulders down. Your voice comes from your posture. Sit hunched and you will sound like you are apologizing.
  5. Drink water. Dry mouth is what makes a voice crack, and a cracking voice is what convinces you that you are failing.
  6. Open by asking, not pitching. Lead with a question so you get to listen for the first two minutes. Your body settles while they talk — and you learn what to actually sell.

Reappraisal finding: Alison Wood Brooks, "Get Excited: Reappraising Pre-Performance Anxiety as Excitement," Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014.

The exposure ladder

Why a ladder. Avoiding the thing you fear is what keeps the fear alive — every time you dodge a call, your nervous system files it as "that really was dangerous." The fix is not confidence first and action after. It is small, survivable exposures in order, repeated until they are boring, and the confidence arrives afterward as a receipt. Three logged reps clear a rung and unlock the next one. You cannot skip.

The script library

Fluency is not talent, it is having the words already loaded. Read these until they are yours, then say them in your own accent.

Opening a cold call
"Hi — this is [your name] with [your business]. I know I'm calling out of the blue, so I'll be quick.— pause, one full second —I take care of [what you do] for properties over on [their street]. Is keeping the front of the building looking sharp something that's on your list this year, or is that not really a priority right now?"

Naming the awkwardness disarms it. Ending on "or is that not a priority" hands them an easy no — and people relax the moment they know they are allowed to refuse. A relaxed person tells you the truth.

The discovery five — questions that make them talk
1. "Walk me through how you handle it now." 2. "What made you start looking at this?" 3. "What happens if nothing changes for another year?" 4. "Besides you, who has to be comfortable with this?" 5. "What would make this an easy yes?"

Target: they talk for seventy percent of a discovery call. If you are doing most of the talking, you are guessing at what to sell. Question three is the one that creates urgency without you manufacturing any.

Saying the price without flinching
"For the full exterior — driveway, walkway, and the front face of the building — it's [one number], and it's done in a day."— then stop talking. Completely. Count to ten in your head. Do not fill the silence, do not add a discount, do not say "but I'm flexible." —

Silence after a number feels like an eternity to the person who said it and like ordinary thinking time to everyone else. Whoever speaks first concedes. If they flinch, do not defend the price — label it: "Sounds like that's higher than you had in mind." Then be quiet again.

The six objections, and what to say back
It's too expensive.
"What number were you expecting?"
You need the size of the gap, not a reason to discount. If you do move, remove scope when you remove dollars — a price that drops for free tells them the first number was fiction.
Just send me something in writing.
"Happy to. So I send you the right thing instead of a generic packet — what's the one part you most need to see: the price, the scope, or the timing?"
Usually a polite exit. The question turns it back into a conversation, and their answer tells you what the real hesitation is.
I need to think about it.
"Of course. In my experience when someone says that, there's one specific thing that isn't sitting right. What is it?"
"Think about it" is never about thinking. It is an unspoken objection you did not surface. Ask warmly and they will usually just tell you.
We already have someone.
"Good — that means you actually value getting it done. I'm not asking you to fire anybody. If they're ever booked out, or you want a second number to compare against, can I check back in ninety days?"
Never attack the incumbent; you insult their judgment for hiring them. Take the calendar date instead — incumbents lose accounts constantly, and the person with the date on file wins it.
Call me next quarter.
"I'll do that. So I don't waste your time — is next quarter a real date, or is it a polite no? Either answer is completely fine."
A clean no is worth more than a maybe. Maybes are what fill a pipeline with ghosts and make you feel busy while you earn nothing.
What makes you different?
"Honestly? Most of us run the same equipment. What's different is that I show up when I said I would, and you get the same person every time — me. Is that the part that's gone wrong before?"
Do not recite features. Admit the commodity, name the one thing you genuinely do better, then hand the question back. The follow-up is where the real story comes out.
Video and conference call mechanics
  • Camera at eye level. A stack of books under the laptop. Shooting up your nose costs you authority before you speak.
  • Light in front of you, window behind the camera. A window behind you turns you into a silhouette, and people do not trust a shadow.
  • Look at the lens, not the face on screen — at two moments specifically: when you say the price, and when you ask for the close. That is what reads as eye contact.
  • Two-second pause after they stop talking. It kills the interrupting that latency causes, and it makes you sound considered rather than eager.
  • Set your display name to your business name, not "iPhone" or "user1."
  • Smile before you speak, not while you speak. Join, smile, then start. The first ten seconds set the temperature of the whole call.
  • Send the agenda beforehand, three lines. Nerves come from not knowing what happens next; an agenda removes that for both of you.
  • Record yourself once a month and watch it. Uncomfortable, and it is the single fastest feedback loop you have access to.
The one-page proposal
  1. Their problem, in their words. One sentence, quoting the phrase they actually used on the call. They should recognize themselves in line one.
  2. The outcome. What it looks like when this is finished — not what you will do, what they will have.
  3. Scope. Three to five bullets. What is included.
  4. What is not included. Nobody does this, and it is the part that builds the most trust — and it is what protects you from scope creep later.
  5. Price. One number. If you must give options, give exactly three and put the one you want them to take in the middle.
  6. Timeline. A start date and a finish date, not "roughly two weeks."
  7. One next step, with a deadline. "Reply 'go' by Friday and I'll hold the 14th for you." A proposal without a deadline is a brochure.

One page. Every extra page lowers the odds it gets read, and an unread proposal loses to a worse offer that got read.

The follow-up that isn't begging
Subject: should I close the file? Hi [name] — I haven't heard back, which usually means one of three things: it isn't a priority right now, you went a different direction, or it's still on the list and the week got away from you. Any of those is fine. Just tell me which one and I'll either close the file or check back in [month]. — [your name]

"Just checking in" makes you sound like you need something. Offering to close the file makes you sound like someone with a full schedule — and it gets replies, because it is easy to answer.
